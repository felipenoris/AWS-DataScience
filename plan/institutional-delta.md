# What a large institution would do differently

Read this when *designing* — it is what keeps a lab compromise from being learned as a pattern.
Not needed while executing a stage.

---

## 11. What a large institution would do differently

Principle 10. Almost every decision in this plan is bent by two constraints a real organization does not
have: a USD 50/month ceiling and a single operator who is also the only user. That is a legitimate way to
build a lab, but it means the environment is not the reference architecture, and the difference should be
learned rather than absorbed by accident. This is the delta, decision by decision.

| Area | This lab | A large institution | Why the difference matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Account structure | One account per environment role; tooling in Production (D14); one account per environment, one data account (D20-D22) and one disposable policy-test account (D29) | Shared Services / Tooling account in an `Infrastructure` OU; a Network account; per-team sandbox accounts; separate Dev/Staging/Prod **per workload**; data accounts **per domain** | The lab still has no boundary between build and runtime, and none between teams. One account per environment role is enough for one team and one workload; at thirty of either, each role becomes a fleet. **Partly closed on 2026-08-09 (D35):** *per-team sandbox accounts* is now the planned shape rather than the delta — `Sandbox` multiplies per business unit, with Stage 14 vending it. What stays a lab compromise is the rest of the row: no Shared Services account, no Network account, **one Development for all business units** (so isolation ends at the graduation boundary and past it is Lake Formation's job), and one Staging/Production for all workloads rather than per workload |
| OUs | Security, Interactive, Data, Workloads, **Policy Test** (D23, D29) | Plus Infrastructure, Deployments, Suspended; Workloads nested into NonProd/Prod | Four of the five carry a real policy set, which is the test an OU must pass; the fifth carries none *by design* — it is where a candidate policy is exercised against a disposable account before it reaches anything real. **This row used to list that OU as the notable absence and it was closed on 2026-08-08 (D29)**, under names chosen to avoid the collision with the Staging account. What is still absent is a `Suspended` OU, which needs accounts to decommission and this lab has none. D23 records the nesting triggers |
| Account vending | Manual Account Factory from the console, by a standing owner, with the post-vend baseline in Terraform (D34) | **AFT** (Account Factory for Terraform) with a customization pipeline, an account request as a merge request, and the baseline applied automatically on vend | **This row's rationale changed on 2026-08-09 and the change is the interesting part.** It used to read "at this handful of accounts, *created once*, automation costs more than it saves" — a premise about frequency, and a false one: the account list is not static, so vending is a standing capability (D34). What still keeps AFT out is measured cost and a dedicated AFT management account of its own, not rarity. At thirty accounts automation is the only way they stay consistent; at ten, a written gate plus a baseline that is already code gets most of it. Lesson 7, applied to frequency instead of price |
| Who may create an account | One identity — Control Tower's own admin user — creates accounts *and* administers Log Archive and Audit, with no approval in front of the act (D34) | Vending requested by a team, approved by cloud governance, executed by a pipeline; the approver, the executor and the auditor are three different people | The lab has one human, so every separation here would be notation. What is real, and stated rather than assumed away: the account-creation authority can also edit the audit trail, and the only things limiting it are MFA, Object Lock in compliance mode, and an alarm on its group |
| Networking | One VPC per account, peered, NAT and endpoints per account | Transit Gateway or Cloud WAN, centralized egress through an inspection VPC, centralized interface endpoints shared by RAM, **IPAM** for CIDR allocation | Peering is O(n^2) and per-account endpoints are the largest hourly cost multiplied by the number of accounts. CIDRs chosen by hand collide eventually |
| VPN | Self-managed WireGuard, peers in a `.tfvars` (D4) | AWS Client VPN or Verified Access, federated to the corporate IdP, with per-user certificates and session logging | Revoking one person here means editing a file and re-applying. That does not survive an offboarding process |
| Identity | Identity Center as the identity source; four users and five groups created by hand (Stage 1b step 2), with the *entitlements* — permission sets, boundaries, group→account assignments — in Terraform | Identity Center federated to the corporate IdP (Entra ID, Okta) via SAML + SCIM, groups driven by HR | Joiners/movers/leavers has to be automatic, or entitlements only ever accumulate. **The seam is already the institutional one** (`plan/conventions.md`, "The identity seam"): people stay in the directory, entitlements are code, and assignments resolve their group by display name — so federating later replaces the directory without rewriting the entitlement plane. What is a lab compromise is only *where the people come from* |
| The alarm over the identity plane | An unfiltered metric filter: **every** Identity Center membership and assignment event fires it (Stage 1b step 8.3, settled 2026-08-09) | The same alarm **filtered to the privileged groups** — Control Tower's own, plus whatever the organization treats as tier-0 — with the SCIM provisioning principal excluded, and routine joiner/leaver traffic going to a log or a SIEM rule rather than to a pager | **This is the row most likely to be copied out of this repository and then quietly muted.** Unfiltered is the *better* choice here and only here: the directory holds six identities, every change in it is worth reading, and a filter keyed on group GUIDs silently matches nothing the day the directory is replaced. At a few hundred data scientists arriving over SCIM the same alarm fires on every onboarding, and an alarm that fires weekly is muted within a fortnight — which is strictly worse than not having built it. **The revision trigger is the arrival of an external identity source, not a headcount**: at that moment resolve the tier-0 group IDs, filter on them, exclude the provisioning principal, and re-test the alarm the way 1b step 8.3 tested the first one |
| Data lake placement | One Data Governance account (D22), producer/consumer separation, LF cross-account sharing as the default read path | Data lake accounts **per domain**, a data-mesh org model, and subscription workflows in front of the shares | This row largely closed on 2026-08-08 — the mechanism is now the institutional one. What remains is scale: one domain and one producer here; an institution has many of each, and the request/approval workflow in front of the share becomes the product |
| Data governance | **Closed 2026-08-08 (D26): the lab adopted it** — a unified domain (DataZone V2) over the hand-built Glue + LF-Tags substrate | SageMaker Unified Studio / SageMaker Catalog (DataZone): business glossary, data products, subscription workflows, lineage | The one row in this table closed by product maturity rather than by budget: official Terraform support (2026-07) removed the practical objection, and the substrate is still built by hand first (Stage 5), so the teaching value survives underneath the portal |
| Who approves access to data | **Closed 2026-08-08: the lab adopted it.** The single `Manager` persona was split into a **Deployment Manager** (`deployment-managers`, lifecycle approvals in the pipeline) and a **Governance Manager** (`governance-managers`, subscription and data-access approvals in the portal, domain owner of the unified domain) | A data steward *per data domain*, sitting inside the producing team rather than centrally, with the subscription queue as their own backlog | The split was first recorded here as notational, then adopted a few hours later — because it is not notational: with one persona a single human writes a job reading restricted data, approves its release **and** approves its data access. What stays a lab compromise is that there is one governance manager for all data, not one per domain |
| Domain topology | One domain, one set of project profiles, all data in one Data Governance account | A domain per business data domain, **decentralised producer accounts** each owning and publishing their own datasets, consumers subscribing across them | This project's Data Governance account is a producer, a consumer and the governance plane at once. That collapse is invisible at one team and becomes the central problem at ten — it is what "data mesh" is a name for |
| Iceberg operations | General-purpose S3 buckets + scheduled Athena `OPTIMIZE`/`VACUUM`, because D13 leans on prefix-level IAM control | **Amazon S3 Tables**: managed Iceberg with automatic compaction, snapshot expiry and Lake Formation integration | The managed service removes the maintenance a hand-rolled lake forgets — but takes away the prefix-level control D13 is built on, so switching is an architecture decision, not a swap |
| Derived data (D19, D31) | Per-principal prefixes with a lifecycle expiry; **a dedicated CMK whose key policy decides who may read the copy**; classification inheritance stated as policy and enforced by nobody | Lineage-aware catalog that propagates the classification of a source onto everything derived from it, plus periodic re-scan of the derived zone | The lab knows *where* copies land but not *what is in them* until Macie says so, days later. Inheritance by policy works with three users and fails silently with thirty |
| Environment promotion | Four environments: Sandbox (experimentation) feeding Development by git, then Development → Staging → Production through the pipeline (D20, D21) | The same chain, plus per-workload triples, and staging data that is a governed, masked copy of production rather than a sample | This row closed on 2026-08-08 in two steps — Staging in the morning, Development and the corrected chain origin in the evening. What stays open is the *data*: sampled data catches permission, schema and wiring errors, and misses everything that only appears at production distribution and volume |
| Access requests | Terraform merge request | Self-service request with approval workflow, time-bound grants | "Ask the platform engineer" does not scale, and permanent grants never get revoked |
| Recovering from a bad guardrail | **No standing exemption** — the policy is detached from the Management account, which is outside SCPs by AWS's design (D16); a bad policy is caught before attachment by the `Policy Canary` battery (D29) | No standing exemption either, plus an incident-scoped role elevated for a bounded window that expires on its own, and a change process in front of the policy set | **This row closed on 2026-08-08, and closed by *removal*.** A standing principal exempt from every custom deny was adopted (D30) and then reverted: it was a permanent hole justified by a hypothetical, which is the shape an institution refuses precisely because the hypothetical is rare and the hole is not. What is still a lab compromise is not the exemption but the *process*: one operator attaching policies by hand, with a canary account instead of a change-management workflow, and no time-boxed elevation path when something does go wrong |
| Egress control | DNS Firewall allowlist, or no internet (`plan/architecture.md` §4.3) | AWS Network Firewall with TLS inspection, plus an internal package mirror covering every ecosystem (Posit Package Manager, Artifactory or similar) | A commercial artifact manager solves in one product what `plan/architecture.md` §4.3 solves with four different fallbacks. It costs money the lab does not have |
| Egress cost | ~USD 0.05/h NAT | ~USD 290/month Network Firewall, accepted without discussion | The lab has to be clever precisely because it cannot buy the obvious answer |
| Shared storage | EFS with Access Points, in **Sandbox only** (D24); POSIX identity not tied to SSO | FSx for Lustre for training throughput, EFS for home directories in *every* interactive account, and file access auditable per user | Two gaps, not one. "Who read this file" is unanswerable in the lab design — in a regulated institution that is disqualifying. And a data scientist working in Development has no shared filesystem at all, so they have to think about which account their files are in; an institution would never make that the user's problem |
| CI/CD trust | Same-account deploy runner with an instance profile (D14; OIDC is blocked because IAM cannot fetch a VPN-only issuer's JWKS) | Build account separate from deploy targets; OIDC federation against a publicly resolvable issuer; signed artifacts; provenance attestation | A compromised runner in the lab compromises Production directly |
| The approval gate itself | GitLab CE: a `when: manual` job on a protected tag, if the edition check in Stage 7 step 3 confirms that group-assigned approvals are Premium; GitLab group membership maintained by hand, mirroring the Identity Center groups | GitLab Premium or Ultimate: protected environments and deployment approval rules as first-class objects, with the approver group driven by SAML group sync from the corporate IdP | The lab's gate constrains *who can push a tag*, which is a proxy for *who approves this release*, not the thing itself. D20's whole argument rests on that gate, so this is the one place where an edition limit reaches a load-bearing control rather than a convenience — and it is why CloudTrail on the two deploy roles (INT-08) is the actual record of what happened |
| When detection is switched on | Free detective controls in the landing zone (IAM Access Analyzer external access, CloudTrail log-file validation, Object Lock); the **metered** ones at the stage that first gives each something to observe — GuardDuty at Stage 4 with the first internet-facing resource, Security Hub at Stage 5 with the first governed data, Macie at Stage 11 | **All of it on day one**, in the landing zone, together with the preventive half — as Control Tower and the AWS SRA lay it out | This is a **cost deferral, and it is a real deviation**, not a refinement. An institution pays for detection over empty accounts without noticing the money, and buys something the lab gives up: no window in which a new account exists un-monitored, and no dependence on anyone remembering. The lab's mitigation is that each service names the *step* that enables it and carries a deliverable there — "later" is a step number, not an intention. The risk this leaves is the one Lesson 5 describes: a control scheduled is not a control enabled, and nothing will complain if Stage 4 step 10 is skipped |
| Who owns the notebook runtime | A **Dev Env Steward** approves the `dev-env` image in a GitLab gate; the build code is a repository the data scientist may write to, so proposing a change is a merge request | A **platform engineering team** owns a catalogue of curated base images, with a published support matrix, deprecation windows, CVE SLAs and an image per team or per workload family | The lab has the *shape* of the control — propose freely, release under one signature — with none of the machinery around it: no deprecation policy, so an approved image stays current forever; no support matrix, so "which image should I use" has one answer by accident; and one steward for every ecosystem, where an institution has an owner per language. The failure mode to watch is the gate becoming a rubber stamp because the approver is also the only person who could have reviewed the diff |
| Backups | AWS Backup + Vault Lock (Stage 12) | The same, plus tested DR runbooks, cross-region recovery, and RTO/RPO agreed per system rather than assumed | The lab tests recovery once; an institution rehearses it |
| Availability | 1 NAT, single-AZ endpoints, single VPN instance (D9, D4) | Multi-AZ everything, no single points of failure | Every availability shortcut here is a deliberate cost trade, and each one is listed in D4 and D9 |
| Operations | One person, `make up` / `make down` | On-call rotation, runbooks, change management, an internal platform team with its own product backlog | The largest difference of all, and the one no amount of Terraform addresses |

**How to use this table.** When a stage is built, check its row here. If the lab approach would be
indefensible at scale, say so in the stage's notes and record what the alternative was — that is the
artefact worth keeping from this project, more than the infrastructure itself.
